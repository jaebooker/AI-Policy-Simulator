    How the model works, and what changed since 2021

    Each quarter

    • Every actor may reconsider joining or leaving the safety bloc. The pull toward cooperation rises with perceived risk, trust in partners, and the research it would gain. It falls with the cost of meeting the shared safety standard and with a lead it wants to protect.
    • Bloc members converge on the treaty safety standard and share research, which lets laggards catch up. Racers let safety erode when a rival is close.
    • Capability compounds. Past the critical research point, AI speeds up its own development.
    • Export controls slow restricted actors and erode their trust in the controllers.

    Deception and verification

    • Some actors are secretly imposters (they take bloc research but skip the safety work) or spies (they race but steal research).
    • Verification catches them, deters them from trying, and partly replaces trust: you need less faith in what you can check.
    • An exposure zeroes trust in the deceiver, locks it out for two years, and dents trust everywhere.

    Endings

    • Warning shots raise everyone's alarm, which then fades. This is the galvanizing event and enforcement gap of the coordination cycle.
    • A catastrophe can strike first. Its odds grow with capability, low safety, and a close race.
    • When someone crosses the threshold, success depends on its true safety level, its margin over the nearest rival outside its coalition, and whether it arrived inside a real coalition.

    Rebuilt from the 2021 version

    • Named actors calibrated to 2026, with editable starting points. The original anonymous setup survives as the Classic scenario.
    • The old model had no notion of risk; AGI was the only ending. Now safety, catastrophe, and alignment are explicit.
    • New mechanics: verification, export controls, trust networks, warning shots, a recursive-improvement threshold.
    • Fixed bugs that made imposters lose progress, spies always log failure, and boolean flags always read true.
    • The Python package and this page share one model and one random generator; a given seed produces the same future in both.
